# CH-001 — A Place of My Own

The elevator made a noise halfway to the fourth floor that did not inspire confidence.

Lily looked up at the metal ceiling as it groaned above her. "That normal?"

Mrs. Bell did not even glance up. She stood beside Lily with one hand on a box marked KITCHEN and the calm expression of someone who had heard the same complaint many times.

"It's been doing that since before you were born."

Lily waited.

Mrs. Bell added, "Probably."

"Much better."

The elevator shuddered once, then continued upward.

Lily tightened her grip on the box in her arms. It contained two mugs, three paperbacks, a desk lamp, a roll of tape, and one shoe. Packing had stopped being a system around two in the morning and become a series of decisions she was no longer willing to defend.

The doors opened with a tired ding.

"See?" Mrs. Bell said. "Perfectly fine."

"Your definition of perfectly is flexible."

That got a small smile.

The fourth-floor hallway was narrow and quiet, with old wooden doors and a long window at the far end. Rain tapped against the glass. Somewhere outside, a tram bell rang twice before fading into Bellweather traffic.

Mrs. Bell stopped at 4B and handed over a brass key.

"There. Yours."

The word hit harder than Lily expected.

Not because the apartment was impressive. It wasn't.

The paint near the ceiling had been touched up in a shade that almost matched. The floor leaned just enough to make a coin roll if it felt ambitious. The kitchen was small, the bathroom smaller, and the living room window looked like it had survived several arguments with the weather.

Still, the key was in Lily's hand.

Hers.

She slid it into the lock. It turned with a little resistance.

Mrs. Bell watched her open the door. "If the radiator complains, hit the valve once. If it complains again, call me. If the elevator stops between floors, press the red button."

"Does that happen often?"

"No."

A pause.

"Often enough to have instructions?"

Mrs. Bell ignored that. "And don't force the built-in cabinet. Old wood swells when it rains."

Lily looked toward the cabinet beside the living-room wall. "Anything else?"

"The fourth-floor laundry line belongs to everyone, which means nobody agrees on the rules. Apartment 4A works nights. The tenant below you has a baby. And if you hear pipes knocking after midnight, it's the heating."

"Very specific."

"People like dramatic explanations for old buildings."

Lily looked around Unit 4B.

"Good thing I'm boring."

Mrs. Bell gave her another sideways smile. "We'll see."

Then she was gone.

The door clicked shut.

For the first time that day, Lily stood alone.

No aunt checking whether she had eaten. No moving company worker asking where a box belonged. No Mrs. Bell explaining which sound was normal and which sound was probably normal.

Just rain at the window and the soft hum of an empty apartment.

Lily put the key on the kitchen counter.

"Okay," she told the room. "Don't be terrible."

The room did not answer.

A promising start.

---

By seven, Lily had learned three things.

First, the floor leaned toward the window.

Second, one of her dining chairs had a shorter leg.

Third, the box marked BATHROOM contained absolutely nothing that belonged in a bathroom.

She sat cross-legged on the living-room floor and held up a frying pan.

"Excellent."

The frying pan offered no apology.

She had managed to make the bedroom usable, which mostly meant the mattress had sheets and the boxes were now arranged around it instead of on top of it. The kitchen had plates. The bathroom had towels after she found them inside a box labeled BOOKS.

Progress.

The living room was still a mess, but a controlled mess.

Lily dragged the small secondhand desk toward the interior east wall. It was narrow enough to fit beside the built-in cabinet and wide enough for her laptop, a notebook, and the desk lamp.

She pushed it into place.

The desk rocked.

Of course it did.

Lily crouched, folded a piece of cardboard twice, and shoved it under one leg.

Stable.

"Professional installation."

Her phone buzzed on top of a box.

Nora: Need help?

Lily looked at the message.

Then at the room.

Then at the stack of boxes that had somehow become more threatening now that she knew exactly how many were left.

She typed, I'm good.

Deleted it.

Typed, Almost done.

That was technically less false.

Nora answered immediately.

You said that two hours ago.

Lily frowned at the screen.

She considered turning the phone face down and pretending she had not seen it.

Instead she wrote: Because I am extremely consistent.

Three dots appeared.

Lily waited.

Nora: Eat something.

Lily looked at the unopened bag of takeout on the counter.

Already did.

She stared at the lie for half a second, deleted it, and replaced it with:

About to.

Nora sent a thumbs-up.

Lily put the phone down.

She ate.

Independence, apparently, included being bullied into dinner from another building.

---

Nora called at eight-thirty.

Lily answered with the phone wedged between her shoulder and ear while trying to fit three mugs onto a shelf designed for two and a half.

"Before you ask, yes, I ate."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were."

"I was going to ask if you found the kettle."

Lily stopped.

Her eyes moved to the kitchen counter.

No kettle.

"That's a different question."

Nora laughed softly. "Check the blue box."

"There are four blue boxes."

"I know."

"You helped pack them."

"I did."

"Then this is partly your crime."

The conversation stayed easy for a while. Nora asked about the elevator, the landlord, whether the lock worked properly. Lily answered. She did not say that hearing Nora's voice made the apartment feel less empty, because that would invite concern, and concern had a way of turning into suggestions.

She found the kettle in a blue box under two winter scarves.

"Why?"

"I got tired near the end," Nora said.

"Good. I thought I had done this."

"You did most of it."

Lily opened her mouth, then closed it.

On the bottom of the box was a ceramic spoon rest shaped like a lemon.

Her mother had bought it during a road trip because she had insisted every kitchen needed at least one object with no serious purpose.

Lily touched the chipped yellow edge with her thumb.

The room went very quiet.

Nora did not fill the silence.

That helped.

Lily set the spoon rest beside the stove.

"There," she said. "Kitchen officially complete."

Nora's voice softened, but not too much. "Looks good?"

Lily glanced around.

The paint still didn't match near the ceiling. One chair still wobbled. Boxes still covered half the floor.

"Yeah."

And, surprisingly, she meant it.

"Staying there tonight?"

"That's usually how apartments work."

"Lily."

"Yes. I'm staying."

Another small silence.

"Okay," Nora said. "Call if you need me."

"I know."

"You can need me and still live there."

Lily looked at the rain sliding down the tall window.

"I know that too."

She almost believed herself.

After they hung up, the ceiling light flickered once.

Lily looked up.

It steadied.

"Old building," she said.

Mrs. Bell would have been proud.

---

At ten-fifteen, Lily decided the final box could remain a problem for tomorrow.

She washed her mug, checked the front door, and turned off the kitchen light.

The living room felt different at night. Smaller, maybe. The lamp on the desk made a warm circle against the east wall while the rest of the apartment settled into shadow.

Rain still tapped against the window.

Lily crossed the room to pull the curtain.

She stopped.

Something outside was wrong.

At first she thought the rain had distorted the glass.

Bellweather should have been there: the building across the street, the tram wires, the pharmacy sign glowing blue on the corner.

Instead, beyond the window, a line of amber-white lights curved through darkness.

An elevated structure crossed the view at an angle that did not exist.

A railcar—or something shaped enough like one for Lily's brain to choose the word—slid past behind a row of narrow metal arches.

She did not move.

The light from outside spilled across her floor.

Not blue from the pharmacy.

Amber.

Her desk lamp dimmed at the same time.

For three seconds, maybe four, Unit 4B felt connected to a city Lily had never seen.

Then the window flashed.

Bellweather returned.

Blue pharmacy sign.

Wet tram wires.

Brick building across the street.

The desk lamp brightened.

Lily stared at the glass.

Her first thought was reflection.

She turned off the lamp.

Nothing changed.

She turned it back on.

Still Bellweather.

Lily checked the latch. Locked.

She pressed her palm to the glass. Cold from the rain.

She stepped to the left, then the right, testing the angle. The street outside remained exactly where it belonged.

No elevated structure.

No amber lights.

No strange railcar.

Lily stood there for a long moment.

The apartment was quiet.

She looked toward the hallway, half expecting someone to explain the trick.

There was no one.

That was almost worse.

Lily pulled the curtain shut.

Then she opened it again.

Bellweather.

"Fine," she whispered.

It was not fine.

She had wanted a place of her own.

For several seconds, it had looked like the place belonged somewhere else.
